# A question helps you notice a useful answer

The same article can leave you with different impressions depending on the problem on your mind. When you are working on reading habits, an example about habits stands out. When collaboration is difficult, you notice an explanation about relationships.

*Feynman’s Questions* provides a place for problems you want to keep exploring. Having them in view helps you connect new information to something that matters to you.

## A question does not need an immediate answer

“How can I use what I read more often?” is unlikely to be resolved by one search. A book may offer a clue; trying a new way of taking notes may offer another.

Write why the question matters. What is difficult now, and what would you like to change? That context helps you recognize useful material when you encounter it.

You do not have to fill a list of twelve questions. One that keeps coming back is enough to start.

## Let experience change the question

When you find something relevant, link it and explain briefly what it contributes. If you see an experiment worth trying, make a project. What happens may make the original question more precise.

Keep track of how your understanding changes. If you develop an idea that you can explain independently, connect it to a permanent note.

Try starting with “How could I…” or “Why does…”. [[_Areas.en]] gives these questions an ongoing place in your reviews; [[_Ideas.en]] holds possible things to try.
