# Bring scattered information into the work you are doing now

You are preparing a presentation, but the material is spread across book notes, saved articles, and an earlier project. Each search breaks the flow of work. You may even search again for something you already found.

A project folder is **a workspace where you bring together what you need to finish a particular piece of work**. Material normally organized by subject belongs here when you are actively using and developing it for the project.

## At the beginning: gather what you already have

Before opening a search engine, look through your notes. Check *Capture* for ideas, *Resources* for relevant knowledge, and *Archives* for similar work. A book chapter or an old outline may already give you a useful start.

**Move material you will work on into the project folder.** If several projects rely on the same reference, it can stay in its existing location with a link from the project’s main note. What matters is being able to reach the materials from the place where you work.

For a four-week reading experiment, gather reading methods, a note template, and the questions that prompted the experiment. Decide how each will contribute. Preparing a project involves selecting and connecting material for the outcome you want.

## During the work: develop the materials and the result together

Compare sources, extract useful parts, and revise your understanding through actual use. Keep experiment results, reasons for decisions, and new questions in the project folder too.

The main note gives you a way into this work. Record what you are trying to do, link the material you are using, and connect the pieces you have made. Longer records can live in separate notes linked from here.

## At the end: give reusable knowledge a new home

Before closing the folder, review its contents. **Move knowledge and materials that remain useful beyond this project to an appropriate subject in Resources.** If a source came from Resources, return it with the improvements you made while using it.

Ongoing responsibilities or standards can continue in Areas. An idea that stands on its own can become a permanent note. Keep the project’s plan, progress, results, and reflection in *Archives*, under `📦Closed-Projects`.

For the reading experiment, the improved method and note template go to Resources. The account of what you tried and what happened stays in the completed project. The next experiment can begin with better materials.

This **Resources → Projects → Resources and Archives** cycle is the core of the folder. The following sections of the main note help you work with the material you have gathered.

## *Twyla Tharp First Memo*: remember why you started

*Building a Second Brain* describes Twyla Tharp’s practice of collecting a project’s materials in a box, beginning with her thoughts and goals for it. This vault treats the project folder as that box.

In the first section of the main note, write why you are doing the work and what you want to achieve:

> I often finish a book without using it in anything else.
>
> I want to try writing the main point and one possible action after reading.
>
> After four weeks, I will review which notes I actually reused and why others went unused.

When the work becomes complicated, return to this memo. It gives you a basis for deciding whether the current activity serves the purpose. Add what you do not yet know and where you might look for answers.

## *Project Progress*: make smaller pieces of useful work

Record what you did, what you noticed, why you made a decision, and which materials helped. Link out to detailed notes as the record grows.

The book’s *Intermediate Packets* provide a useful way to approach a large task. A source summary, a small experiment, or a rough outline can already be a piece of work you use in the next stage. In the reading experiment, a weekly comparison or a simple note template can serve that role.

An *Archipelago of Ideas* starts with the material you already have. Lay out relevant notes, decide an order, and connect them into an explanation.

As the project grows, *Reduce the Scope* to something you can finish. You can test one reading method with your current tools before building a new tool as well. Keep the deferred idea somewhere you can return to it.

## *The Hemingway Bridge*: leave a place to pick up next time

Write this **before you stop a work session**, not only when the whole project is over.

> I compared two note formats, but have not measured how long they take to use.
>
> Next, choose two passages of similar length and try one format with each.
>
> Measure reading time separately from note-writing time.

These lines preserve the current state, the unresolved question, the next action, and a condition you might otherwise forget. They make it easier to recover the context after a few days away.

**The first memo gives you direction; the progress record shows the current state; The Hemingway Bridge gives you a next action.**

## To start now

Choose Project in the Basic template to create the project folder and main note. Write why you are doing it and what would count as finished. Gather the relevant material you already have. Leave a next action when you stop, and redistribute the material when the project ends.
