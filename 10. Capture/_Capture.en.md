# A good thought will not wait until you are ready to organize it

An idea on a walk, a question in conversation, a sentence you notice in a book: any of these can disappear while you tell yourself you will write it down later. Even choosing a folder can be enough to lose the thread.

*Capture* gives you a place to start. **Write enough to keep the thought; decide what to do with it when you review it.**

## What is worth keeping?

The capture practice in *Building a Second Brain* pays attention to what resonates: something surprising, useful for a problem you face, or difficult to explain but hard to stop thinking about. You do not need to save everything that might someday be useful.

Along with the material, leave a sentence about why it caught your attention:

> This article might help with the problem of finishing a book but being unable to explain it. I want to try the method next time I read.

Later, that sentence lets you recover your interest and question as well as the source.

## Two ways in

For a thought of your own, use [[_Zettelkasten-FleetingNotes.en]]. For something you have read or watched, use [[_Zettelkasten-ReferenceNotes.en]] and keep the source. The Basic template’s Idea and Reference choices follow this distinction.

If one note contains both, distinguish the source’s claims from your own response. There is no need to interrupt a thought just to classify it perfectly.

## Make time to return

Capture works when you come back to it. During a weekly review, open a few notes and decide whether they belong with a current project, in *Resources*, or with a question you want to keep exploring.

Add a short *Distill* summary once you understand the material. [[_Organize.en]] explains where notes can go, and [[_Periodic-Notes.en]] provides a rhythm for reviewing them. For now, write one thing you want to remember and why you want to remember it.
